Two ideas that are often confused

Eman 0:00 – 5:00

Separate legal personality

Who is the party?
  • The company owns property, contracts, sues and is sued in its own name
  • Its rights and debts are its own, not its members'
  • Exists even with one dominant shareholder

Limited liability

How much can an investor lose?
  • A member's exposure is capped at their capital contribution
  • Unpaid company debts do not become personal debts
  • Makes passive investment and risk-taking possible
The trade-off: investors get a capped risk, and creditors carry what is left. The veil is the price the law pays for investment, so it lifts it only for good reason.

Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd

[1897] AC 22 (HL)
Eman 0:00 – 5:00
1

Facts

Aron Salomon sold his boot business to a company he formed. He held 20,001 of 20,007 shares and secured debentures.

2

Dispute

The company became insolvent. The liquidator argued it was only Salomon's agent, so he should pay its debts.

3

Held

The company was validly formed and a separate person. Salomon was not liable, and ranked as a secured creditor.

Why it still matters  Control by one person does not defeat separate personality. This is the baseline both systems start from: in Saudi Arabia, Article 9 of the Companies Law gives a company legal personality on registration in the Commercial Register.

Saudi Arabia: personality starts at registration

Companies Law, Royal Decree No (M/132) dated 1/12/1443H, in force 19 January 2023
Monerah 5:00 – 10:00
Exposure zone: founders act before the company fully exists
Registration in the Commercial Register → legal person
Article 9

Personality on registration

A company acquires legal personality on registration. During incorporation it has personality only as far as incorporation needs. If incorporation is not completed, the persons who acted in its name are jointly and personally liable to third parties.

Article 8

Duty to register documents

The persons responsible must register the company's documents and amendments. If they fail to, they are jointly and severally liable for any damage that results.

Saudi Arabia: liability that reaches outsiders

Monerah 5:00 – 10:00
Arts 26–27

Managers and board members owe duties of care and loyalty, and may not hold conflicting interests or compete without approval.

Art 28

Jointly liable to compensate the company, partners, shareholders or third parties for breach of the Law or articles, or error or negligence. Exclusion clauses are void.

Art 242(2)

Members who resolve to dissolve when assets cannot cover debts become jointly liable for the outstanding debts.

Art 209

Partners in a professional company are personally liable for their own professional errors.

The Sharia layer

Saudi courts apply Sharia principles that forbid harm and fraud. In theory this gives judges room to reach misuse the statute does not list (Vogel, 2000). In practice it is hard to measure, because few commercial judgments are published.

Key point: Article 28 lets third parties claim directly against managers and board members. UK law offers outsiders no equivalent route outside insolvency.

United Kingdom: statute does most of the work

Insolvency Act 1986: personal liability for your own conduct, with the veil left intact
Monerah 5:00 – 10:00
s 213

Fraudulent trading

Who: any person knowingly party to the business

Test: business carried on with intent to defraud creditors, or for any fraudulent purpose

s 214

Wrongful trading

Who: directors and shadow directors

Test: knew, or ought to have concluded, that insolvent liquidation could not reasonably be avoided. Defence: took every step to minimise creditors' loss

Remedy  the court orders a contribution to the company's assets for all creditors. Only available once the company is in insolvent liquidation.

Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd

[2013] UKSC 34 · Divorce: London properties held by companies Mr Prest controlled
Monerah 5:00 – 10:00

Concealment principle

The court looks behind the company to find the real actors. This is not piercing: the company's separate personality is respected.

Evasion principle

Piercing only where a person under an existing legal obligation deliberately evades it through a company they control. Last resort only.

Outcome  The veil was not pierced: evasion did not apply. But the companies held the properties on resulting trust for Mr Prest, so they went to his wife through ordinary trust law. A different remedy did the job and the company stayed separate.

Your turn: pierce or not?

Decide for each system, then tap a card to flip it.

1

The land deal

Ahmed agrees to sell you land, then transfers it to a company he owns so he doesn't have to complete.

Flip ↻
United Kingdom
YES

Evasion principle. An existing duty evaded through his company: Jones v Lipman. Specific performance against the company.

Saudi Arabia
LIKELY

No general piercing article. The claimant relies on Sharia prohibitions on fraud and judicial discretion. Plausible, less predictable.

2

The overseas subsidiary

Workers harmed by a foreign subsidiary sue the parent company because the subsidiary has no money.

Flip ↻
United Kingdom
NO

Adams v Cape: a group is not one economic unit, and using subsidiaries to limit future liability is legitimate.

Saudi Arabia
NO

Shareholders liable only up to their contributions. The parent is exposed only if it acted as manager or board member (Art 28).

3

Signed too early

Founders sign a supply contract in the company's name before registration. The company is never registered.

Flip ↻
United Kingdom
PERSONAL

Companies Act 2006 s 51: whoever contracts for an unformed company is personally liable. Not piercing: there is no company yet.

Saudi Arabia
PERSONAL

Article 9: those who acted in its name are jointly and personally liable to third parties.

4

Trading into the wall

The board keeps trading for six months after it should have seen insolvent liquidation was unavoidable.

Flip ↻
United Kingdom
CONTRIBUTE

Wrongful trading, IA 1986 s 214. Directors contribute to the company's assets for all creditors. Veil intact.

Saudi Arabia
PERSONAL

Article 28: managers and board members jointly liable for negligence, and third parties can claim directly.

Critical evaluation

Nouran 10:00 – 15:00

United Kingdom

+  Clear, predictable test after Prest

+  Investors can price their risk in advance

–  Evasion is hard to prove

–  Tort victims and later creditors are left exposed

–  Directors face outsiders mainly in insolvency

Saudi Arabia

+  Objective triggers; no need to prove bad intent

+  Art 28 gives outsiders a direct claim

–  No general anti-abuse rule beyond listed provisions

–  Sharia discretion is hard to predict

–  Few published commercial judgments

Our view  the UK buys certainty with a narrow remedy. Saudi Arabia gives outsiders more routes against directors, but its broader judicial discretion is less transparent. Publishing commercial judgments would let investors and creditors see where the Saudi line sits.

Conclusion

Nouran 10:00 – 15:00
1

Same foundation

Both treat the company as a separate person, with limited liability as the norm.

2

Different exceptions

The UK pierces only for deliberate evasion. Saudi law lists objective triggers in statute.

3

Different targets

Most real liability falls on directors through statute: IA 1986 in the UK, Art 28 in Saudi Arabia.

The question is never whether the veil exists.
It is who carries the cost when it is misused.
